This document defines the governance model for determining where directory information comes from, what evidence supports an assertion, how verification is represented, how conflicting or changing information is handled, and how canonical public information is protected from unsupported claims.

It complements MOD-003, which establishes the broader information authority and provenance model. It does not amend or override any approved foundation, architecture, URL, taxonomy, information, or geography decision.

---

## 1. Purpose

The Institutes & Education Directory will depend on information collected from many sources over a long period. Sources may differ in authority, completeness, freshness, purpose, and reliability.

This specification establishes durable rules for:

- identifying and classifying sources;
- preserving evidence for material assertions;
- distinguishing source authority from verification state;
- distinguishing confidence from verification;
- handling primary, secondary, tertiary, submitted, discovered, and AI-assisted information;
- resolving conflicting assertions;
- recording freshness and review history;
- correcting and re-verifying information;
- preserving historical evidence and temporal context;
- governing automated monitoring and change detection;
- preventing derived information from becoming an uncontrolled source of truth;
- determining when information may become canonical public directory information.

The objective is not to guarantee that every published fact is permanently correct. The objective is to create a traceable, reviewable, and correctable information-governance system.

---

## 2. Governing Principles

### MOD-SRC-001 — Evidence before authority

A claim must have identifiable supporting evidence before it can be treated as a governed information assertion.

A source being available does not automatically make every statement within that source authoritative.

### MOD-SRC-002 — Source authority is not the same as truth

Source classification describes the role and authority of a source. Verification describes the review state of a specific assertion.

A highly authoritative source can contain outdated information. A lower-authority source can provide useful evidence that helps identify a change or inconsistency.

### MOD-SRC-003 — Verification applies to assertions

Verification is evaluated at the level of a material information assertion or logically related group of assertions, not merely at the level of an institution or source.

An institution may therefore have:

- verified identity;
- verified address;
- partially verified program information;
- unverified fee information;
- historical evidence for a former campus;
- current information under review.

### MOD-SRC-004 — Canonical publication requires governance

Information must not become canonical public directory information merely because it was discovered, scraped, submitted, generated by AI, or found in a widely used third-party source.

Canonical publication requires the applicable evidence and verification conditions defined by this specification and related approved specifications.

### MOD-SRC-005 — Preserve uncertainty

When evidence is incomplete or conflicting, the directory must preserve the uncertainty rather than silently selecting an unsupported value.

### MOD-SRC-006 — Derived data does not become upstream authority

Search indexes, SEO pages, maps, rankings, summaries, recommendations, AI representations, and other derived outputs must not silently become sources of canonical institutional information.

### MOD-SRC-007 — Historical truth remains traceable

When information changes, previous material assertions and their supporting evidence should remain historically traceable where appropriate. Updating the current value must not erase the reason that the previous value existed.

## 3. Scope of Governed Information

This specification applies to material information associated with:

- Institution identity;
- official and alternate names;
- institution type and classification;
- legal or operational status;
- campuses and physical locations;
- geographic assignments;
- addresses and postal information;
- coordinates;
- contact information;
- programs;
- program offerings;
- admissions information;
- affiliations and accreditation;
- scholarships and financial information;
- facilities and services;
- official web and digital channels;
- other future directory attributes designated as material.

The same governance principles may later be extended to reviews, ratings, user contributions, admissions notices, merit information, and other modules through dedicated specifications.

## 4. Source Classes

Sources are classified by their role, authority, and relationship to the asserted information.

### 4.1 Primary or authoritative sources

These are sources with direct institutional, statutory, regulatory, governmental, or otherwise authoritative responsibility for the information.

Examples may include:

- competent government records;
- official regulatory registers;
- official gazettes or statutory notices;
- recognized accreditation or regulatory records;
- official institutional records;
- official institutional websites or publications where appropriate;
- formal institutional communications;
- authoritative geographic or postal records.

The presence of an official source does not eliminate the need to evaluate freshness, scope, and whether the source actually supports the specific assertion.

### 4.2 Secondary sources

Secondary sources provide useful corroboration, contextual information, or independent evidence but are not necessarily the controlling authority.

Examples may include:

- recognized educational bodies;
- examination or affiliation authorities;
- inspection or accreditation reports;
- established educational publications;
- reputable institutional directories;
- documented partner or affiliation records.

### 4.3 Tertiary and discovery sources

Tertiary sources can assist discovery and corroboration but generally require additional verification before supporting canonical publication.

Examples may include:

- general web directories;
- search results;
- public mapping layers;
- social profiles;
- news articles;
- user-maintained websites;
- aggregators;
- discussion forums;
- cached or copied information.

These sources may be valuable signals without being authoritative.

### 4.4 Institution-submitted information

Information submitted by an institution is explicitly attributable to that institution.

Institution-submitted information must not be represented as independently verified merely because the institution supplied it.

Its governance state must distinguish:

- submitted;
- reviewed;
- verified against independent or authoritative evidence;
- accepted with stated attribution;
- rejected or superseded.

### 4.5 Contributor or user-submitted information

Contributors and users may identify errors, changes, missing institutions, or new evidence.

Such submissions are evidence or change proposals until appropriately reviewed. They must not silently modify canonical information.

### 4.6 Web-discovered information

Information discovered through crawling, browsing, search, public web pages, or automated discovery is treated as a candidate assertion until evaluated under the applicable source and verification rules.

Discovery is not verification.

### 4.7 AI-generated or AI-assisted information

AI may identify candidate institutions, extract facts, detect possible changes, normalize information, suggest classifications, or identify conflicts.

AI output is a derived proposal unless independently supported and governed through the same evidence and verification process as other information.

AI must not be treated as an authoritative source merely because its output appears plausible.

## 5. Source Authority Dimensions

Source authority should be evaluated using more than a simple source ranking.

Relevant dimensions include:

1. **Institutional authority** — whether the source is responsible for the fact.
2. **Legal or regulatory authority** — whether the source has statutory or regulatory responsibility.
3. **Directness** — whether the source directly describes the asserted fact.
4. **Specificity** — whether the source addresses the exact institution, campus, program, or offering.
5. **Freshness** — how recently the source reflects the relevant state.
6. **Continuity** — whether the source is maintained over time.
7. **Independence** — whether the source is independent from another source being used as corroboration.
8. **Evidence quality** — whether the underlying material clearly supports the assertion.
9. **Scope** — whether the source applies to the relevant jurisdiction, campus, program, or period.
10. **Traceability** — whether the source can be retained or revisited sufficiently to support auditability.

No single dimension should automatically determine verification in every circumstance.

A material assertion should be traceable to one or more evidence items.

Evidence may include:

- official documents;
- official webpages;
- regulatory records;
- gazette notices;
- institutional prospectuses;
- institutional communications;
- inspection reports;
- geographic records;
- postal records;
- dated announcements;
- submitted documents;
- field verification records;
- structured source extracts;
- archived historical material;
- other approved evidence types.

### MOD-SRC-009 — Evidence must retain context

Evidence should preserve enough context to determine:

- what was observed;
- where it came from;
- when it was observed or published;
- which assertion it supports;
- the relevant institution, campus, program, or offering;
- the source's applicable scope;
- whether it represents current or historical information.

### MOD-SRC-010 — Evidence is not automatically canonical

Retaining evidence does not mean the evidence itself is published as directory truth.

The directory evaluates evidence before promoting an assertion into the canonical information layer.

## 7. Verification States

The directory should distinguish meaningful verification states rather than using a single verified/unverified flag.

A conceptual lifecycle may include:

1. **Discovered** — information has been identified but not yet reviewed.
2. **Submitted** — information was explicitly provided by an institution, contributor, or user.
3. **Under Review** — evidence is being evaluated.
4. **Corroborated** — supporting evidence exists from one or more sources.
5. **Verified** — the applicable review process has established sufficient support for the assertion.
6. **Conditionally Accepted** — information is suitable for a defined use but has a stated limitation or attribution requirement.
7. **Conflicted** — credible sources disagree and resolution is pending.
8. **Stale** — previously accepted information has exceeded its applicable freshness expectation or is otherwise due for revalidation.
9. **Superseded** — the assertion was valid for an earlier period or has been replaced.
10. **Rejected** — evidence was insufficient, contradicted, invalid, or otherwise unsuitable.
11. **Withdrawn** — a previously submitted or proposed assertion has been intentionally withdrawn.

The exact implementation states and transitions require later technical specification.

---

## 8. Verification versus Confidence

### MOD-SRC-011 — Verification and confidence are separate concepts

**Verification state** answers:

> Has the applicable review process established sufficient support for this assertion?

**Confidence** answers:

> How strongly does the available evidence support the assertion relative to uncertainty and conflicting information?

An assertion may be verified for publication while still having a stated confidence or limitation, and an apparently high-confidence prediction or AI extraction must not be treated as verified without the required evidence review.

The project must not use confidence as a substitute for verification.

---

## 9. Verification Scope

Verification should consider:

- identity match;
- source authority;
- evidence directness;
- freshness;
- temporal applicability;
- geographic applicability;
- campus applicability;
- program applicability;
- contradiction from other credible sources;
- materiality of the assertion;
- potential impact of publishing an incorrect value.

Higher-risk or higher-impact information may require stronger evidence or additional review.

---

## 10. Freshness and Review Cycles

### MOD-SRC-012 — Freshness is field-sensitive

Not all information becomes stale at the same rate.

Examples:

- institution name and legal identity may change infrequently;
- contact details can change more frequently;
- admissions deadlines can change rapidly;
- fees can change by academic session;
- program availability can change between intakes;
- campus locations may change less frequently but have high importance when they do.

Future implementation must therefore support information-specific freshness expectations rather than assuming one universal expiration period.

### MOD-SRC-013 — Staleness does not equal falsehood

An assertion that has become stale is not automatically false.

Staleness means that the information requires renewed review before it should continue to be treated as current under the applicable policy.

### MOD-SRC-014 — Review cycles are governed

Future operational specifications should define review frequencies or event-driven revalidation rules appropriate to information type, source, and risk.

---

## 11. Temporal Validity

Material information should be understood in relation to time.

The directory must be capable of distinguishing:

- effective date;
- observed date;
- publication date;
- verification date;
- review date;
- expiry or supersession date where applicable.

A current value and a historical value may both be correct when evaluated within their respective time periods.

This is especially important for:

- institution names;
- legal status;
- campus locations;
- administrative geography;
- programs;
- affiliations;
- admissions information;
- fees;
- contact details.

This requirement complements MOD-005's geographic temporal model.

---

## 12. Conflicting Sources

### MOD-SRC-015 — Conflicts must be preserved and resolved deliberately

When credible sources disagree:

1. preserve the competing assertions and their sources;
2. identify the exact point of disagreement;
3. evaluate source authority and applicability;
4. compare freshness and effective periods;
5. determine whether the conflict is temporal rather than factual;
6. seek additional evidence where necessary;
7. document the resolution;
8. retain the historical conflict for auditability when material.

The directory must not silently overwrite one credible assertion with another without recording why.

### MOD-SRC-016 — No universal source hierarchy

Source authority should be evaluated in context.

For example, an official institution website may be the appropriate source for a current contact number, while a statutory regulator may be the appropriate authority for legal recognition or accreditation status.

A source hierarchy must therefore be attribute-sensitive rather than an unconditional global ranking.

---

## 13. Institution-Submitted Information

Institution submissions can provide important current information, especially where official public records are incomplete.

However:

- attribution must remain clear;
- submission time must be recorded;
- the submitter's authority or role may require evaluation;
- supporting documentation may be requested;
- material claims may require independent verification;
- accepted information may carry an explicit attribution or limitation.

Institution-submitted information must never be presented as independently verified when it has only been self-reported.

---

## 14. User and Contributor Submissions

User or contributor reports can support:

- correction suggestions;
- missing institution discovery;
- location changes;
- broken links;
- outdated contact information;
- evidence of institutional changes.

Submissions should enter a review workflow and should not directly mutate canonical information without appropriate controls.

Abuse prevention, moderation, identity requirements, rate limits, and privacy rules require later dedicated specifications if public submissions are enabled.

---

## 15. Web Discovery and Automated Collection

Automated discovery can improve coverage and identify potential changes at scale.

However:

- discovery output is candidate information;
- extraction errors must be expected;
- source identity must be retained;
- timestamps must be preserved;
- automated collection must not silently promote unverified values to canonical status;
- important changes should be subject to appropriate review.

Scraping, crawling, synchronization architecture, and implementation are outside the approval scope of this document.

---

## 16. Change Detection

### MOD-SRC-017 — Change detection identifies candidates

Automated or manual change detection may identify:

- changed institution names;
- new or removed campuses;
- address changes;
- changed contact details;
- program additions or removals;
- admission notices;
- changed regulatory status;
- changed source content.

A detected change is a review signal, not automatically a canonical change.

### MOD-SRC-018 — Materiality determines review depth

The project should distinguish between:

- cosmetic or low-impact changes;
- routine factual changes;
- material institutional changes;
- changes affecting legal identity, accreditation, status, safety, or public trust.

Future operational governance should define proportionate review requirements.

---

## 17. AI-Assisted Monitoring

AI may be used to:

- discover candidate institutions;
- extract structured facts from sources;
- compare source versions;
- identify possible conflicts;
- summarize evidence;
- suggest classification;
- identify likely stale records;
- propose corrections;
- prioritize records for human review.

AI output remains derived information.

### MOD-SRC-019 — AI cannot self-authorize canonical truth

An AI-generated claim must not become canonical solely because:

- the model is highly capable;
- multiple AI systems agree;
- a confidence score is high;
- automated extraction appears consistent;
- the claim is plausible.

The claim requires supporting evidence and the applicable verification process.

---

## 18. Correction Workflow

A correction should conceptually follow:

1. **Identify** — a possible error or change is reported or detected.
2. **Capture** — preserve the new evidence and source context.
3. **Assess** — determine whether the issue is material and what information is affected.
4. **Verify** — evaluate the new evidence.
5. **Resolve** — determine the current canonical assertion.
6. **Record** — preserve the decision, evidence, timestamps, and historical context.
7. **Publish** — update approved public representations.
8. **Propagate** — update governed derived representations.
9. **Validate** — confirm that the correction is reflected correctly.
10. **Close or monitor** — retain follow-up requirements if uncertainty remains.

---

## 19. Re-Verification

Re-verification may be triggered by:

- scheduled review;
- source changes;
- conflicting evidence;
- institutional submission;
- user correction;
- regulatory update;
- detected website change;
- relocation;
- program or admission cycle changes;
- major geographic changes;
- other material events.

Re-verification should preserve prior verification history rather than resetting the record without explanation.

---

## 20. Stale Information Handling

When material information becomes stale:

- do not silently present it as current if policy requires current verification;
- distinguish stale information from known false information;
- consider retaining historical value for context;
- trigger re-verification where appropriate;
- avoid unnecessary deletion of useful historical evidence;
- ensure derived search and SEO representations do not misrepresent stale data as current.

Public display rules for stale information require later SEO/UI/content specifications.

---

## 21. Source Retirement and Link Failure

Sources can disappear, move, become inaccessible, or stop being maintained.

The directory should preserve:

- source identity;
- source type;
- last observed date;
- relevant evidence captured under applicable policy;
- historical relationship to assertions;
- reason for source retirement or unavailability where known.

A broken source link does not automatically invalidate an assertion if sufficient historical evidence and independent support remain available.

---

## 22. Canonical Publication Rules

An assertion may become canonical public information only when:

1. the assertion is clearly scoped;
2. its source/evidence is identifiable;
3. its verification state satisfies the applicable publication requirement;
4. temporal applicability is understood;
5. material conflicts are resolved or explicitly represented;
6. attribution and limitations are preserved where necessary;
7. the assertion does not depend solely on an unsupported derived representation.

Canonical publication is a governed outcome, not an automatic consequence of ingestion.

---

## 23. Derived Representations

The following are derived consumers of governed information:

- search indexes;
- filters;
- nearby results;
- maps;
- SEO landing pages;
- structured data;
- comparison views;
- rankings;
- recommendation systems;
- AI summaries;
- notifications;
- analytics;
- external APIs where approved.

Derived representations must be traceable to their governed upstream information and must have rules for invalidation or refresh when upstream information changes.

---

## 24. Auditability

Material information changes should be traceable to:

- who or what introduced the assertion;
- source and evidence;
- observation/submission time;
- review and verification events;
- reviewer or governing process where applicable;
- resulting decision;
- previous and new values when material;
- reason for change;
- applicable effective period.

Auditability supports correction, accountability, quality review, historical research, and long-term maintenance.

---

## 25. Data Quality Dimensions

Source and verification governance should support at least these quality dimensions:

1. **Accuracy**
2. **Completeness**
3. **Consistency**
4. **Freshness**
5. **Provenance**
6. **Verifiability**
7. **Temporal correctness**
8. **Scope correctness**
9. **Non-duplication**
10. **Traceability**
11. **Conflict visibility**
12. **Correction recoverability**

A record can be strong in one dimension and weak in another. Quality must therefore not be reduced to a single score.

---

## 26. Materiality

Not every assertion requires identical governance effort.

Materiality should consider:

- effect on student decisions;
- legal or regulatory significance;
- safety implications;
- financial implications;
- SEO/public visibility;
- risk of misleading users;
- frequency of change;
- reversibility of publication;
- potential downstream propagation.

Future implementation specifications may define formal materiality classes.

---

## 27. Source and Evidence Retention Direction

The project should preserve enough source and evidence context to support:

- later verification;
- correction;
- historical reconstruction;
- audit;
- dispute handling;
- quality analysis.

Retention periods, storage mechanisms, legal holds, privacy requirements, and deletion rules require later dedicated specifications and applicable legal review.

---

## 28. Governance of Derived AI Knowledge

AI systems may build secondary representations from directory data.

These representations must remain linked conceptually to their source data and should be regenerable when canonical information changes.

AI summaries, embeddings, classifications, recommendations, or extracted knowledge must not become irreversible replacements for canonical source records.

---

## 29. Long-Term Governance

The source and verification model must remain usable as:

- the number of institutions grows;
- new source types appear;
- new countries or regions are introduced;
- new data providers become available;
- AI capabilities change;
- automated monitoring becomes more capable;
- human editorial teams change;
- technology providers are replaced.

The governance principles should remain stable even when implementation mechanisms change.
